Transcript
You're watching this video, thanks to computer chips, that started their life on silicon wafers like this one. But for future electronics and quantum technologies, we're exploring materials that are much, much thinner, sometimes just one molecular layer thick. These materials are called van der Waals materials or two-dimensional materials. And their properties can vary across regions barely the width of a single human hair. We're introducing to you in this tutorial a technique called imaging spectroscopic ellipsometry, specifically on the case of van der Waals materials. It is a non-contact optical technique that uses polarised light that is reflected off from the sample of interest and the polarisation changes in the reflected light are measured. Two quantities, Psi and Delta, describe relative reflection amplitude changes and wave phase differences. An imaging detector gives us on top of that at each pixel these two parameters preventing an averaging of our area or sample of interest with its environment. And then repeating these measurements across different wavelengths builds a whole hyperspectral data set. To however extract actual thicknesses or optical constants from this measurement, we need to use an optical model. We need to fit this optical model of the entire stack to our ellipsometric data. And there, unfortunately, different assumptions can produce similarly good fits. This is why we need to calibrate the optical model and constrain it as much as we can with previously known measurement parameters. For example, we can measure thicknesses with atomic force microscopy and fix them in our optical model. And we can use Raman spectroscopy to identify the actual chemical nature of the materials on our sample. Each of these techniques measure different properties. Small spreads in fitted values cannot really reveal how uncertain we were with our optical model. Our aim is to teach you that you can obtain numbers easily with ellipsometry but you really need to know when you can trust them.
